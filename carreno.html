Classic etiquette, 1853

The Carreño Rules

The 1853 etiquette manual that raised generations of Latin American families. His table rules, in our words, with a tip for today.

Source: Carreño, Manual de urbanidad y buenas maneras (1853), chapters "De los banquetes" and "De la mesa."
A

At a formal lunch or dinner

Seats are assigned

Tap a seat.

Hosts (H) sit at the center, facing each other.

Source: Carreño, "De los banquetes."

Tap a rule to open it.

The host sets the pace

Sit after the host, start eating when the host does, and rise only when the host rises.

Napkin and posture

Napkin on your lap at once, used only to dab your lips. Sit upright, elbows off the table, legs still, both hands in sight.

A steady pace

Neither rushed nor slow. Take enough to show you enjoy the food, go easy on drinks, and never press drinks on anyone.

Ask, don't reach

Never stretch across a neighbor or stand up to grab something. Ask the person beside you or the server.

Pleasant conversation

Keep it light and positive. No arguments, heavy topics or bad news. Speak softly, with the people near you.

Quiet mouth

No talking with food in your mouth or to someone who is drinking. No loud chewing, slurping, or blowing on or sniffing your food.

Never criticize the food

Show no disgust. At formal tables, don't overdo the praise either: one sincere compliment is enough.

Toasts are special

Save toasts for formal occasions and leave them to those prepared. Never put anyone on the spot.

Linger after dessert

When the hosts rise, everyone moves on, often to coffee. Don't leave right after the meal; stay a while.

At a restaurant? The same banquet manners apply. Whoever invited you is the host: follow their lead, from the first seat to the last sip.

B

The things on the table

Tap a rule to open it.

Everything has a place

Bread on the left, glasses and cups on the right. Leave them where they were set.

Knife cuts, fork carries

Hold both lightly, not by the very end. The knife only cuts and serves; it never goes to your mouth.

The spoon's job

Liquids and foods a fork can't hold. Fill it modestly, and keep cutlery from clicking against your teeth.

Bread by hand

Break off one small piece at a time and keep crumbs over your plate. Classic rule: no bread until the soup is finished.

Glasses

Hold a glass near its base, a wine glass by the stem. Drink quietly, eyes on the glass. Dab your lips before and after.

Salt and sauces

Put them on the plate beside your food, not on the rim. Take shared salt, sauce or sugar with the serving spoon.

Pause vs. finished

Pausing: knife and fork crossed on the plate, handles on the rim. Finished: side by side on the plate.

Once touched, it's yours

Never pass or offer food or drink you have already touched or tasted.

Bones and seeds

Move them discreetly to the side of your plate, and avoid them in the first place when you can.

Source: Carreño, "De la mesa," sections on the table in general and on serving.
C

Servers and companions

Tap a rule to open it.

Speak kindly to servers

Low, calm and polite: neither overly familiar nor harsh. Ask the server, not your neighbors, for what you need.

Spills happen

Hosts never scold staff in public. If something spills or breaks, stay kind and unbothered.

Plan ahead

A host gives instructions to the staff before the meal, not in the middle of it.

Neighbors first

Offer and pass dishes to the people beside you before helping yourself. Give others the best parts.

Accept graciously

Don't make anyone repeat an offer. Accept politely, or at least taste.

Bring a good mood

Be cheerful, sober and discreet, even when tired. Leave complaints and private matters at home.

Source: Carreño, "De la mesa" and "De los banquetes."
